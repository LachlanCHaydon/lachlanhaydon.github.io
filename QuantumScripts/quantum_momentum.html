DE BROGLIE WAVELENGTH
A plane wave has definite momentum. Its momentum-space representation is a perfect delta function at p = ℏk. Higher momentum → more rapid spatial oscillation → shorter de Broglie wavelength. Notice the real (cyan) and imaginary (pink) parts — the wave spirals in the complex plane. The probability |ψ|² is completely flat — the particle could be anywhere. Definite momentum means completely undefined position.

Why the ground state can't have zero momentum: For a particle in a box, the wavefunction must be zero at the walls. A zero-momentum state would be completely flat — it can't satisfy the boundary conditions. So the wavefunction must curve, and that curvature is momentum. Higher n means more wiggles → higher spatial frequency → more momentum. Notice the momentum distribution is always symmetric around zero — ⟨p⟩ = 0, meaning the particle is equally likely to be "moving" left as right. The spread Δp grows with n and shrinks with L.

Superposition = time evolution. Unlike a single eigenstate (which is stationary), a superposition of energy eigenstates evolves in time — the probability cloud "sloshes" back and forth. This is because different energy components accumulate phase at different rates: e^{-iEₙt/ℏ}. Watch how both the position and momentum distributions change together. For a pure eigenstate, everything is frozen — the distributions are time-independent.
